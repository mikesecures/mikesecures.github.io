The Origin of Proxy

My portfolio needed a little life, so I originally fired up Gemini Canvas to code a sleek, glowing 3D monogram out of particles. It was technically cool, but it lacked personality.

Since I'm never afraid to change direction, and a little weird, I pivoted: "Actually, let's make it an octopus."

Gemini Canvas started crunching parametric equations, spawning tens of thousands of independent particles to form a volumetric mantle and eight sweeping tentacles. But a static octopus is just a statue. To get him swimming without frying anyone's browser, we bypassed standard animation frameworks and injected the physics directly into custom WebGL vertex shaders. By offloading the complex math straight to the GPU, his tentacles began to rhythmically undulate and his head began to "breathe" at a flawless 60 frames per second.

I threw in some floating "bubbles" for a deep-sea vibe and gave him a fun party trick: poke him with your cursor, and his cool blue bioluminescence smoothly swells into a fiery, angry orange. He was moving kind of erratically when interacted with, so I tweaked his spatial awareness. Now, if you drag him around to look him in the eyes, he doesn't awkwardly snap back to a default pose—he just holds your gaze while drifting through the void.

I named him Proxy.

Today, he lives natively on my site, rendered entirely in raw code without a single image file or 3D model. He just floats there gracefully between you and my application—a glowing, slightly judgmental guardian keeping an eye on my web traffic.
